There's a moment in every project where the numbers stop being numbers.

A column of air-quality readings is just noise until you see that one city
breathes clean most of the year and chokes for three weeks every winter. A
model's accuracy score is just a decimal until you ask *which* people it gets
wrong. That moment — where measurement turns into meaning — is the part I care
about most. And I've come to believe it's the actual job.

So here's my vision, plainly: **a data scientist's real job is translation.**
Not just building models, but turning what the data knows into something a
person can hold. The model is rarely the hard part. The translation is.

## Why I work this way

Most data work ends up locked behind two walls. The first is jargon — language
that makes simple findings sound complicated. The second is the dashboard dump —
a screen with forty numbers and no story, where everything is shown and nothing
is said.

I'm trying to do the opposite. I want a project that a curious teenager can open
and *get*, while a researcher in the field still finds it rigorous and honest. I
don't think those two goals fight each other. Clear isn't the same as dumbed
down. Clear is harder.

## The three lenses

This site is called 3P for a reason. Three disciplines keep showing up in how I
work, and together they're basically my method:

**Physics** taught me to reason from first principles and to sanity-check the
size of an answer before trusting its decimals. A clean answer to the wrong
question is still wrong.

**Psyche** — psychology — keeps reminding me that data describes *people*. When a
dataset is about sleep, or therapy outcomes, or how someone learns a language,
"accuracy" is never the only thing worth measuring. Who it helps and who it
misses matters just as much.

**Python** is where ideas become runnable. It's the thread that lets me actually
try something instead of just thinking about it — and, just as importantly, lets
other people open it up and inspect it.

## How it shows up in my work

This isn't a theory I hold off to the side. It's how I build.

In [AirVision]({{ '/projects/' | relative_url }}), I took satellite and weather
data for fifteen Pakistani cities and spent most of my effort *not* on the
numbers, but on the layer between the data and the reader — so you could open a
city and feel the difference between a good day and a bad one.

In my work on therapy and sleep classifiers, the interesting questions weren't
about squeezing out another percent of accuracy. They were about the human
behind each label, and about being honest when the data was too small or too
messy to trust.

## What I'm building toward

I want to keep doing exactly this, at a higher level each time — taking work that
would normally stay in a notebook or a research paper and making it something
people can read, question, and use. Not flashier. Clearer. More honest.
